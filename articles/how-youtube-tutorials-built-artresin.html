How YouTube Tutorials Built ArtResin's Customer Base

Nobody buys a two-part chemical from a stranger. That's the single most useful thing I can tell you about marketing a product like epoxy resin.

When my partner and I were building ArtResin, we figured that out early. Artists were nervous about resin. It's a chemical process. There are ratios to get right, bubbles to deal with, surfaces to prep, cure times to respect. A lot can go wrong, and when it does, the instinct is to blame the product. So before we ever thought about paid ads or influencer campaigns, we asked ourselves a simpler question: what are artists actually typing into search?

The answers were things like "how to mix epoxy resin," "why is my resin cloudy," "how to use a torch on resin," "can I pour resin over acrylic paint." Those weren't abstract marketing opportunities. They were real people, at their kitchen tables, scared they were about to ruin a piece they'd spent weeks on. We could either leave them to figure it out alone, or we could show up and help.

We chose to show up.

Education Is Marketing People Thank You For

The YouTube tutorials we built were straightforward. We showed the full process: measuring, mixing, pouring, using a torch to pop bubbles, finishing the edges. We showed what a good pour looked like. We also showed what could go wrong and why. That last part mattered more than anything else.

Showing real failures was not a risk. It was the whole point. When you show someone the mistake and explain exactly what causes it, you do two things at once. You teach them how to avoid it, and you signal that you actually know what you're talking about. Trust doesn't come from polished demos where everything works perfectly. It comes from honesty about the messy parts.

The product was always the tool in those videos, not the star. The star was the artist's work. The resin just had to get out of the way and let the painting shine. That framing kept the content useful rather than promotional, and useful content gets watched, shared, and searched again.

We paired the video work with e-newsletters and social content that followed the same philosophy. Answer the question the customer is already asking. Don't make them feel dumb for not knowing. Invite them into the process.

The Support Cost Nobody Talks About

Here's a business case that doesn't get discussed enough in marketing conversations: education reduces support costs.

Most bad reviews for a product like ours didn't come from a bad product. They came from user error. Someone mixed the ratio wrong, or didn't stir long enough, or poured in a cold garage. The result was a sticky, tacky, or cloudy surface, and they were understandably frustrated. If they found us through a tutorial first, they already knew those pitfalls. The tutorial was doing support work before the problem ever happened.

Fewer failures meant fewer frustrated customers. Fewer frustrated customers meant fewer bad reviews. Fewer bad reviews meant better conversion for everyone who found us later. The educational content wasn't just marketing. It was quality control happening at scale, out in public, for free.

The Playbook, Kept Simple

If I were handing this approach to someone starting a product-based business today, I'd keep the playbook to a few principles.

First, answer the questions customers actually type into search. Not the questions you wish they were asking. The real ones, including the nervous ones and the beginner ones. Those are your content briefs.

Second, show real failures. Tutorials that only show success feel like ads. Tutorials that show the thing going wrong and then explain why feel like a friend who's done it a hundred times.

Third, make the product the tool, not the star. The person watching wants to make something beautiful. Help them do that. The product is just how they get there.

Fourth, be consistent. One great video does something. A library of great videos does something else entirely. It becomes a resource people bookmark, return to, and recommend. That's when the compounding starts.

This philosophy didn't stay inside ArtResin. It carried into how I think about all my work now. My own YouTube channel at youtube.com/@davezak runs on the same idea. Around 13,000 subscribers who found me because I was trying to be useful, not promotional. That's a small number by platform standards, but it's an audience that actually watches and actually trusts.

The Person Who Taught You

The deepest truth underneath all of this is pretty simple. People buy from the person who taught them. Not because of loyalty in some abstract sense, but because teaching earns trust, and trust is what converts a curious browser into an actual customer.

We built ArtResin's customer base one tutorial at a time, one honest answer at a time, one artist helped at a time. It was slow in the beginning and then it wasn't. That's how compounding works.
