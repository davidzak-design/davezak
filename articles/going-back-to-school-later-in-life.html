Going Back to School Later in Life: Learning to Learn Again

There is a specific discomfort that comes with being genuinely bad at something, and most of us spend a lot of energy avoiding it. Once you have built real expertise, once you know how things work and how to get results, starting over feels almost offensive. Why would you go back to not knowing?

I have had to answer that question a few times now, and every time the answer has been the same: because the not-knowing is the whole point.

Expertise Has a Shadow Side

When you get good at something, really good, a quiet rigidity can move in alongside the competence. You stop questioning certain assumptions because you stopped needing to years ago. You reach for proven tools automatically. Your pattern-matching gets faster and faster, which is genuinely useful, until it isn't. The phrase that starts to live in the back of your head is: this is how it's done.

I trained as an artist and animator. That foundation gave me a visual language I still draw from every day. You can read more about that arc on my bio page. But somewhere along the way I noticed that expertise in one medium could become a kind of ceiling. The more fluent you are in a language, the harder it is to imagine the sentence differently.

The antidote, at least for me, has always been to switch languages entirely.

Three Times I Had to Start from Zero

The first time was the training itself: learning to draw, to animate, to think visually from first principles. Humbling, slow, and completely necessary.

The second time was founding ArtResin. I was not a chemist. I was not a manufacturer or a supply chain person or a regulatory expert. I was an artist who had a problem with the products available and decided to do something about it. Building that company meant entering a world where I was, without question, the least knowledgeable person in the room on the technical side. I partnered with chemists rather than pretending to be one. I asked obvious questions without embarrassment. I learned the vocabulary of an industry I had never worked in, piece by piece, until I could hold my own. The full story of that journey is on the timeline.

What ArtResin taught me was that beginner status is actually a kind of permission. You can ask things that experts would never ask, because experts already know the answer, or think they do. Some of the most useful questions I asked in those early days were ones no one in that industry had thought to ask in years, simply because the answer seemed obvious to them.

The third time was more recent, and in some ways the most uncomfortable: learning to code. My son and I decided to build a browser game together called STONK! Neither of us was a developer going in. I have written about that whole experience in detail in a separate article, but the short version is that I spent a lot of time being genuinely confused, genuinely stuck, and genuinely proud of small wins that any experienced developer would consider trivial. It was exactly the kind of learning that middle-aged expertise tries to protect you from, and it was exactly what I needed.

What Learning to Learn Actually Means

Re-entering a beginner state is not about abandoning what you know. It is about keeping the muscle loose. There is a difference between expertise and calcification, and the gap between them is whether you have practiced being wrong recently.

When you are learning something genuinely new, a few things happen. You become more patient with confusion. You get better at separating what you actually understand from what you have only memorized. You remember that most skills feel impossible until suddenly they don't, which makes you more willing to stay in the uncomfortable middle.

Those are not just virtues in a classroom. They are survival skills right now.

We are in a moment where the tools are changing faster than the expertise built on top of them. Motion design, illustration, writing, coding, strategy: every field I work in is being rewritten at the foundation level by AI tools that did not exist in their current form a few years ago. Yesterday's workflow expertise has a shorter shelf life than it used to. The people who are going to navigate this well are not necessarily the ones with the deepest existing expertise. They are the ones who are most comfortable becoming a beginner again, quickly, without their ego getting in the way.

The meta-skill, the ability to learn how to learn, is the one that compounds across every medium switch and every era of tools. It does not expire.

The Practical Part

I am not going to tell you to enroll in a course or follow a curriculum, partly because that has rarely been how I have learned anything important. What I would say is this: find something you genuinely do not know how to do yet, something that feels slightly embarrassing to admit you can't do, and start. Let yourself be bad at it on purpose. Notice when your existing expertise tries to take shortcuts that aren't actually available in this new domain. That friction is the work.

The artists I find most interesting, the entrepreneurs, the designers, the people doing work that surprises you, almost all of them have this quality. They are not afraid of the beginner feeling. They might even be chasing it a little.

I know I am.
