Why Entering an Industry With No Experience Can Be an Advantage

When I decided to start ArtResin, I had no chemistry degree, no background in industrial coatings, and no idea how epoxy resin was actually manufactured. What I did have was a clear, frustrated point of view as an artist who kept using resins that yellowed, smelled toxic, and required a hazmat suit to apply safely. That frustration turned out to be worth more than a chemistry PhD.

It sounds counterintuitive. We are conditioned to believe that credentials and deep industry knowledge are the price of admission to any serious venture. And to a point, that is true. But there is a flip side that rarely gets discussed: sometimes the person who has been inside an industry for twenty years is the last person who can see what is broken about it.

The Outsider Sees the Obvious

Insiders accumulate assumptions the way ships accumulate barnacles. After enough years, those assumptions just feel like physics. "That's how epoxy works." "Artists will deal with the fumes." "Non-yellowing formula at this price point isn't viable." Nobody questions them because everybody absorbed them at the same time, from the same sources, inside the same professional culture.

When I walked into the resin space, I hadn't absorbed any of that. I asked what probably sounded like naive questions. Why can't this be genuinely non-toxic? Why does it have to yellow over time? Why is every product clearly designed for industrial contractors and not for someone coating a painting on a kitchen table? Those questions weren't sophisticated. But they were honest, and the honest answer to each of them turned out to be: there is no good reason. The incumbents just hadn't been asked.

The outsider advantage is basically this: you are not anchored to how things have always been done, so you are free to ask whether things need to be done that way at all.

What I Actually Did About It

Here is the part that matters, because the outsider advantage only works if you are honest about what you don't know. I did not pretend to be a chemist. I thought like an artist and I partnered with real chemists to execute. My job was to hold the user's perspective, to keep asking "but would an artist actually want this?" and to refuse to let the formulation drift toward industrial defaults out of habit. Their job was to make the chemistry work safely and correctly.

That division of responsibility was the whole thing. I brought the lens. They brought the expertise. The product that came out of that collaboration resonated with artists in a way that the incumbent industrial epoxy makers had completely missed, not because I was smarter than the chemists, but because I was asking a different question. I was designing for a person, not for a specification sheet.

You can read more about how that whole journey unfolded in my longer piece on how I built ArtResin.

The Principle Scales Further Than You'd Think

Once I noticed this dynamic, I started seeing it everywhere. Take a field like small modular nuclear reactors. It is a space dominated by deeply credentialed engineers and legacy institutional culture. Imagine someone with a background in commercial product design and go-to-market strategy deciding to start a company there. On paper, absurd. But consider what they might actually bring: a clear eye on what regulators, investors, and end users actually need to see and hear; design thinking applied to the customer experience around permitting and deployment; commercial instincts that pure engineering culture sometimes lacks.

I want to be clear: this is a hypothetical, purely to illustrate the principle. I am not starting a nuclear company. But the pattern is identical to what happened with ArtResin. An outsider who respects the genuine technical and safety constraints, and who hires real experts to handle what requires real expertise, can sometimes reframe the problem in a way insiders cannot. The key phrase is "respects the constraints." There is no outsider cleverness that overrides physics, chemistry, or safety regulation, and pretending otherwise is how people get hurt or embarrassed. The move is to bring your fresh angle to the commercial and conceptual layer while deferring to expertise on the technical layer.

When to Trust Your Naivety

Not every industry is equally open to this. The more commoditized, assumption-laden, and user-neglected a space is, the more an outsider perspective is worth. The more the technical barriers are genuinely existential, the more your outsider angle needs a strong expert partner to mean anything at all.

ArtResin worked because artists were a real and underserved user group, the chemistry problem was solvable with the right partners, and nobody inside the epoxy industry was paying attention to that corner of the market. All three conditions had to be true. I got lucky that they were. But I also looked for them deliberately, even if I couldn't have articulated that process at the time.

If you are sitting on an idea in a field where you have no formal background, I would not let that stop you from taking it seriously. Do your homework. Find people who know what you don't. Be genuinely humble about the gaps. But do not assume that the people already inside the industry have thought of everything. Sometimes the most valuable thing you can bring is the fact that nobody told you it couldn't be done.
